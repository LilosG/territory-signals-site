This guide summarizes official pages as they read on November 26, 2026. It is not legal or planning advice. Rules in this area have been amended and will be amended again, so confirm any parcel with the City and a qualified professional.

## What do SB 9 and SB 79 change?

SB 9 and SB 79 are two different state laws that can each allow more homes on some parcels than local zoning alone would. They work on different land for different reasons, and mixing them up is the fastest way to give a client bad information.

| Question | SB 9 | SB 79 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main idea | Two homes and a lot split on single-family lots | Denser housing near qualifying transit |
| Where it starts | Single-family residential zones | Sites zoned residential, mixed-use or commercial near specified stops |
| Distance to transit matters | Not for basic eligibility | Yes, it sets the standards |
| Typical scale | Up to two primary homes per lot | Multi-unit projects, with a five-unit minimum in the City's summary |
| Approval | Ministerial | Building permit process, with other permits still possible |

## What do SB 9 and SB 79 allow on a single-family lot?

Of SB 9 and SB 79, it is SB 9 that speaks directly to the single-family lot. The Department of Housing and Community Development (HCD) explains that SB 9 requires ministerial approval of a housing development with no more than two primary units in a single-family zone, the subdivision of such a parcel into two parcels, or both. Ministerial review means staff check the application against objective standards, without a discretionary hearing. HCD's April 2026 fact sheet says a complete application must be approved or denied within 60 days of receipt.

The key points an agent should know:

- **Zone.** The parcel must be in a single-family residential zone, within an urbanized area. A multifamily or mixed-use zone that merely permits a single house does not count under SB 9.
- **Lot split.** The split creates two lots of roughly equal size. Local standards cannot be used to stop the creation of two lots of 1,200 square feet each.
- **Home size.** Local objective standards cannot prevent homes of at least 800 square feet.
- **Setbacks.** Side and rear setbacks can be required only up to four feet, with an exception for existing structures and rebuilding on the same footprint.
- **Parking.** A city may require up to one off-street space per unit, and none in certain locations near transit or car share.
- **No short-term rentals.** Rentals created through SB 9 must be for terms longer than 30 days.
- **Owner intent on lot splits.** An applicant for a lot split signs an affidavit that they intend to live in one of the units as their principal residence for at least three years from approval. Community land trusts and qualifying nonprofits are treated differently.
- **One split only.** A parcel created by an SB 9 split cannot be split again under it, and a city need not approve splitting a lot next to one that the same owner, or someone acting with them, already split.

The affidavit matters commercially. A buyer who plans to split and sell both halves without living there needs advice before writing an offer, not after.

**What about accessory units?**

SB 9 and state accessory dwelling unit law work together, but the counting differs. HCD's 2024 fact sheet explains that after an SB 9 lot split, a city must allow two units on each resulting lot, and accessory and junior units count toward that two. Without a split, accessory units are treated under accessory dwelling law in addition to the SB 9 homes. HCD states that SB 9 never requires a city to allow more than four units on the original lot area.

## Which parcels do SB 9 and SB 79 rules leave out?

Both SB 9 and SB 79 leave out many parcels that look eligible on a map, and the exclusions are where deals fall apart. For SB 9, HCD lists, among others:

- Projects that would demolish or alter housing restricted by covenant or law to affordable rents.
- Projects affecting housing under rent or price control.
- Projects affecting housing that a tenant has occupied in the last three years.
- Parcels where units were withdrawn from the rental market under the Ellis Act in the last 15 years.
- Contributing structures in historic districts, listed historic resources and certain locally designated historic property.
- Specified environmentally sensitive, hazardous or protected land, drawn from a list in state law. HCD's examples include wetlands, farmland, fire hazard areas, earthquake hazard areas, flood risk areas, conservation areas and wildlife habitat. Some categories carry their own conditions and exceptions.

A city may also deny a project if its building official makes written findings of a specific adverse impact on health, safety or the physical environment that cannot be feasibly mitigated. HCD describes that as a high threshold.

For SB 79, the City's July 2026 bulletin says a project is not allowed if it would demolish more than two rent- or price-controlled units occupied within the past seven years, or where such a demolition already happened in that window, or on a mobile home park site.

Notice what several of these have in common. Tenancy history, rent control status, past withdrawals from the rental market and historic status do not appear in a zoning layer. A map screen cannot see them.

## How do SB 9 and SB 79 transit rules work in San Diego?

Of SB 9 and SB 79, the transit rules that change building scale belong to SB 79. HCD reports that SB 79 was signed on October 10, 2025, took effect on July 1, 2026 and sits in Government Code sections 65912.155 to 65912.162. It makes qualifying transit-oriented projects an allowed use on residential, mixed-use and commercial sites near specified transit stops in certain urban counties. Regional planning agencies map the stops and zones by tier.

The City of San Diego's bulletin places the City's eligible stops in Tier 2 and describes these standards by distance:

| Distance from a qualifying stop | Maximum height | Maximum density | Maximum floor area ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Within 200 feet | 85 feet | 140 units per acre | 4.0 |
| Within a quarter mile | 65 feet | 100 units per acre | 3.0 |
| More than a quarter mile, within half a mile | 55 feet | 80 units per acre | 2.5 |

The same bulletin sets other conditions. A project needs at least five units and a density of at least 30 units per acre or the base zone minimum, whichever is greater. Average unit size is limited to 1,750 net habitable square feet. Projects of more than ten units must include affordable homes, at 7 percent for extremely low income, 10 percent for very low income or 13 percent for lower income households. City dwelling unit protection rules apply. In the Coastal Overlay Zone, SB 79 does not remove a requirement for a Coastal Development Permit.

**Phasing is the part to check every time.**

San Diego did not switch SB 79 on everywhere at once. The City says that on July 1, 2026 it took effect on about 24 percent of the total area. Under the City's ordinance:

| Sites | Status as the City describes it |
| --- | --- |
| Very high fire hazard severity zones | Deferred until the City adopts its Transit Village Plan |
| Sites with designated historic resources as of January 1, 2025 | Deferred until that plan |
| Sites subject to one foot of sea level rise | Deferred until that plan |
| Low resource areas on the state opportunity map | Deferred to 2031 |
| Areas more than a one-mile walk from a qualifying stop | Exempt |

The City has said it aims to bring the Transit Village Plan, its alternative plan under SB 79, to the City Council in early 2027. The City lists 74 qualifying stops after a June 18, 2026 regional map that it describes as a draft, while also calling the City's stop list final. Where official pages leave room for doubt, do not resolve it yourself. The City's bulletin says SB 79 is effective only at the locations on its eligible sites layer in the Zoning and Parcel portal. Check that layer for the specific parcel.

## How do you screen parcels for SB 9 and SB 79 potential?

You screen for SB 9 and SB 79 potential in two passes: a wide map-based pass that finds candidates, then a parcel-by-parcel pass that tries to disqualify each one. Expect the second pass to remove many.

**Pass one: find candidates.**

For SB 9, join parcels to base zoning and keep those in single-family base zones with enough area for two lots of at least 1,200 square feet. For SB 79, measure from each parcel to qualifying rail and bus stops and sort into the three distance bands.

Before They Sell runs this kind of screen, and it is open about what the screen is. Its SB 9 screen keeps parcels in the City's RS single-family base zones with lots of at least 2,400 square feet. It leaves planned-district zones out entirely, so some single-family neighborhoods show no estimate at all. Its SB 79 screen uses public transit stop and route files that carry no service frequency or dedicated-lane information, so it identifies rapid bus stops by proxy and does not apply the law's frequency or lane tests. It also does not apply the City's deferrals and exemptions. That means a candidate can appear inside an SB 79 distance band and still not be eligible today.

Every result is stored and shown as **estimated eligibility**, with a deliberately modest match confidence that reduces its weight in the score.

**Pass two: try to disqualify each parcel.**

Work through the following before you say anything to an owner or a buyer.

1. **Zone.** Confirm the current base zone, any planned district and any overlay.
2. **City layers.** For SB 79, find the parcel on the City's eligible sites layer. Check deferrals and exemptions.
3. **Walking distance.** Straight-line distance and walking distance are not the same. The City's exemption uses walking distance.
4. **Historic status.** Check state and local listings, including whether a structure contributes to a district.
5. **Hazards and sensitive land.** Fire, flood, fault, wetland and habitat constraints.
6. **Coastal.** Determine whether the parcel is in the Coastal Overlay Zone and which permits still apply.
7. **Tenancy and rent history.** Ask the owner. Records will not tell you enough.
8. **Prior splits.** Check this parcel and its neighbors.
9. **Easements, access, utilities and slope.** A legal split can still be impractical.
10. **Feasibility.** Cost, financing and market value decide whether anything gets built.

Write down what you checked, where and on what date. If the parcel clears every step, the next step is a planner, a land-use attorney or the City, not a marketing claim.

## How should agents talk about SB 9 and SB 79 potential?

Agents should talk about SB 9 and SB 79 potential as a possibility to investigate, never as a right the property is known to have. Words such as "qualifies", "approved for" or "zoned for six units" can create liability if they are wrong, and a screen cannot make them right.

With a long-time owner, a useful offer is a side-by-side: what the property would likely sell for as a home, and what might be worth finding out about its value as a lot. Say plainly that the second figure depends on eligibility that has not been confirmed. With a builder or investor, share property facts and say which checks have and have not been done.

Good practice in either direction:

- Label every eligibility statement as estimated, in writing.
- Name the official source and the date you looked.
- Do not publish an address as eligible without confirmation.
- Protect the owner. A candidate list does not mean anyone wants to sell. A share page from Before They Sell carries neighborhood, lot size, zoning, estimated upzoning types and agent contact, with no address or owner details.
- Include the license number and responsible broker's name that California requires on first-contact materials.

A screen is most valuable when it saves you from the wrong conversations. Rank candidates by what else is true: a lot well above the area's median size, a long holding period, a demolition or map approval already in the permit record. In Before They Sell the [Builder bait recipe](/recipes/builder-bait) requires all of an upzoning estimate, a lot at least one and a half times the area's median residential lot and twenty or more years of ownership.

## What should you verify before relying on SB 9 and SB 79?

Before relying on SB 9 and SB 79 for any parcel, verify the current text and the City's current layers, because both have moved recently. HCD's April 2026 fact sheet notes that SB 450, enacted in 2024, tightened limits on local standards under SB 9, and that AB 1061, enacted in 2025, clarified the historic protections. The City's SB 79 treatment depends on an ordinance, a regional map and a plan that had not been adopted when this guide was written.

Keep a short checklist for each file: the date of the fact sheet or bulletin you relied on, the City layer you checked, the screenshot or export you saved and the professional you consulted. If more than a few months have passed, check again before acting.

For agents who farm a territory, the practical gain is focus. Instead of reading every parcel, you read the candidates, remove the ones that fail and spend your time on the few where a real conversation about land value makes sense.
